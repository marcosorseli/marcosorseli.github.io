    Making sense of Itô's Lemma

    My goal for this post is to build an intuitive feel for Stochastic Differential Equations (SDEs) and solve a few examples, both on paper and with code. I am going to skip the heavy measure theory and focus strictly on the computational mechanics. Down the line, I plan to look at fun results like Kakutani’s theorem, which bridges random walks to PDEs. But first, we need to see exactly why classical calculus breaks down when we introduce noise and how Itô's Lemma fixes it.

    Brownian Motion

    The main ingredient we need, before talking about SDEs or Itô's Lemma, is Brownian motion. It will be responsible for the noise (or the word stochastic) in SDEs. Brownian motion arises when we take infinitely many small steps in a random walk. More precisely, suppose you are stepping up or down according to the result of a fair coin flip. At each step \(k\), let the step direction be:

    \[ \xi_k = \begin{cases} +1, & \text{with probability } 1/2 \\ -1, & \text{with probability } 1/2 \end{cases} \]

    Each step has zero mean and unit variance:

    \[\mathbb{E}[\xi_k] = (+1)\frac{1}{2} + (-1)\frac{1}{2} = 0\]
    \[\text{Var}(\xi_k) = \mathbb{E}[\xi_k^2] - (\mathbb{E}[\xi_k])^2 = 1 - 0 = 1\]

    After \(N\) steps of size \(\Delta x\), your position is:

    \[X_N = \Delta x \sum_{k=1}^N \xi_k\]

    Let's look at the basic statistics of this position:

    Expected position:

    \[\mathbb{E}[X_N] = \Delta x \sum_{k=1}^N \mathbb{E}[\xi_k] = 0\]

    Because each coin flip is independent, the variance becomes:

    \[\text{Var}(X_N) = (\Delta x)^2 \sum_{k=1}^N \text{Var}(\xi_k) = N (\Delta x)^2\]

    This gives an average distance from the origin (standard deviation) of \(\Delta x \sqrt{N}\).

    A Random Walk

    Now suppose that we want the number of steps in our random walk to go to infinity. More precisely, suppose we have a random walk that takes \(N\) steps in a second, one every \(\Delta t = 1/N\) seconds. What happens if we send \(N\) to infinity?

    Notice that if we keep the length of our steps fixed at \(1\), the path would blow up completely. Taking infinitely many steps of length \(1\) would send the walker infinitely far away. To obtain a continuous limit path, the step size \(\Delta x\) must shrink as \(N\) grows.

    Now the question is, how fast must \(\Delta x\) shrink? To answer that, let's look at the variance formula \(\text{Var}(X_N) = N (\Delta x)^2\).

    If we shrink steps linearly with time (\(\Delta x \sim \Delta t = 1/N\)), the variance becomes:

    \[\text{Var}(X_N) = N \cdot \left(\frac{1}{N}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{N} \xrightarrow{N \to \infty} 0\]

    The random walk collapses into a flat line at zero.

    If we keep the steps fixed (e.g., \(\Delta x = 1\)):

    \[\text{Var}(X_N) = N \cdot 1 \xrightarrow{N \to \infty} \infty\]

    The variance goes to infinity, breaking the path, as we discussed before.

    The balance point must be somewhere in between. To keep the variance finite and non-zero after one second (say, \(\text{Var}(X_N) = 1\)), we must have:

    \[N (\Delta x)^2 = 1 \implies \Delta x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} = \sqrt{\Delta t}\]

    Brownian motion arises from a process just like this. We take a random walk and make the steps more and more frequent. Taking into account our last discussion, to have a continuous limiting path that neither flattens to zero nor blows up, we need the step size to scale as \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\). As we send the time between steps to zero (\(\Delta t \to 0\)), we obtain a fractal-like continuous path.

    From random walk to Brownian motion Steps: 10

    As you can see in the image, the resulting path when we drag the button all the way to the right, is a jagged, fractal-like, line. While Brownian motion (which we denote by \(W_t\)) can be obtained via this limiting process, it is also characterised by the following properties:

    1. Starts at zero: \(W_0 = 0\).
    2. Continuous paths: The function \(t \mapsto W_t\) is continuous everywhere.
    3. Independent increments: For any time steps \(0 \le t_1 < t_2 < \dots < t_n\), the increments \(W_{t_2} - W_{t_1}, W_{t_3} - W_{t_2}, \dots\) are completely independent. Where it goes next doesn't depend on how it got there.
    4. Gaussian steps: The change over any time interval \([s, t]\) is normally distributed with mean 0 and variance \(t-s\). That is, \(W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t-s)\).

    Proving that such an object actually exists is a bit tricky, so we will take its existence for granted.

    Note that it follows straight from the definitions that \(E[W_t]=0\) and \(Var(W_t)=t\) for all \(t\) (since \(W_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t)\)).

    Calculus for Noisy Paths

    In calculus, to understand the behaviour of a function we have the tools of differentiation and integration. We would like to have something similar for functions involving noise, which for us means involving Brownian motion. Before we add noise, let's remember how classical calculus works. Suppose we have a simple, linear Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE):

    \[ dx_t = \mu x_t dt \]

    This just says that the change in \(x\) over a tiny time step \(dt\) is proportional to its current value. To solve this, we rely on the chain rule.

    Let's apply the natural logarithm function, \(f(x) = \ln(x)\), to our variable. By the classical chain rule, the change in our function is \(df = f'(x) dx\). Since the derivative of \(\ln(x)\) is \(1/x\), we get:

    \[ d(\ln x_t) = \frac{1}{x_t} dx_t \]

    Now we just substitute our original ODE (\(dx_t = \mu x_t dt\)) into this equation:

    \[ d(\ln x_t) = \frac{1}{x_t} (\mu x_t dt) = \mu dt \]

    Suddenly, the \(x_t\) cancels out completely! We are left with \(d(\ln x_t) = \mu dt\). Integrating both sides is now trivial: \(\ln(x_t) - \ln(x_0) = \mu t\). Exponentiating both sides gives us our solution: \(x_t = x_0 e^{\mu t}\).

    The Problem with Noise

    But what happens if our system involves noise? We add our Brownian motion \(W_t\) into the mix to represent the noise. Now our equation looks like what we call a Stochastic Differential Equation (SDE):

    \[dx_t = \mu x_t dt + \sigma x_t dW_t\]

    Just like before, this describes the change in \(x\) over a tiny time step \(dt\). But now, that change is made of two parts: a predictable percentage drift (\(\mu dt\)), and an unpredictable percentage shock (\(\sigma dW_t\)) driven by our Brownian noise.

    If we want to solve this, our first instinct is to use the exact same classical chain rule trick we used for the ODE. Let's apply \(f(x) = \ln(x)\) and see what happens.

    By classical calculus, \(d(\ln x_t) = \frac{1}{x_t} dx_t\). Substituting our SDE into this gives:

    \[d(\ln x_t) = \frac{1}{x_t} (\mu x_t dt + \sigma x_t dW_t) = \mu dt + \sigma dW_t\]

    The \(x_t\) cancels out again! Integrating both sides gives us \(\ln(x_t) - \ln(x_0) = \mu t + \sigma W_t\). Exponentiating both sides leads us to what seems like the exact solution:

    \[x_t = x_0 e^{\mu t + \sigma W_t}\]

    This equation allows us to calculate some important quantities related to the paths driven by this SDE. Because \(W_t\) is a Brownian motion, it is normally distributed with a variance of \(t\), \(W_t\sim \mathcal{N}(0,t)\). The moment generating function of \(W_t\) is \(M(s)=\mathbb{E}[e^{sW_t}] = e^{\frac{1}{2}ts^2}\). Plugging \(s=\sigma\) we get \(\mathbb{E}[e^{\sigma W_t}]=e^{\frac{1}{2}t \sigma^2}\) Using this, we can compute the expected mean of a path given by our formula:

    \[\mathbb{E}[x_t] = \mathbb{E}[x_0 e^{\mu t + \sigma W_t}] = x_0 e^{\mu t}\mathbb{E}[e^{\sigma W_t}] = x_0 e^{(\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t}\]

    We can use the same generating function trick to compute the variance and standard deviation. Using the identity \(\text{Var}(x_t) = \mathbb{E}[x_t^2] - (\mathbb{E}[x_t])^2\), we first calculate the second moment by plugging \(s = 2\sigma\) into our moment generating function:

    \[\mathbb{E}[x_t^2] = \mathbb{E}[(x_0 e^{\mu t + \sigma W_t})^2] = x_0^2 e^{2\mu t} \mathbb{E}[e^{2\sigma W_t}] = x_0^2 e^{2\mu t} \cdot e^{\frac{1}{2}t(2\sigma)^2} = x_0^2 e^{2\mu t + 2\sigma^2 t}\]

    Then we subtract the squared expectation we found earlier:

    \[\text{Var}(x_t) = x_0^2 e^{2\mu t + 2\sigma^2 t} - \left(x_0 e^{(\mu + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t}\right)^2 = x_0^2 e^{2\mu t + 2\sigma^2 t} - x_0^2 e^{2\mu t + \sigma^2 t} = x_0^2 e^{2\mu t + \sigma^2 t} (e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1) = (\mathbb{E}[x_t])^2 (e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1)\]

    Taking the square root gives us the standard deviation:

    \[\text{StdDev}(x_t) = \mathbb{E}[x_t] \sqrt{e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1}\]

    Before we declare the equation as solved, let us test it numerically. We can simulate the true solution of the SDE by discretizing our time interval and walking forward one tiny step at a time. Starting at our initial value \(x_0=1\), to figure out where we are a fraction of a second later (after a time \(dt\)), we just look at our equation: we add a drift term (\(\mu x_t dt\)) to a noise term (\(\sigma x_t dW_t\)).

    Using this method we obtain the path our SDE describes step by step (\(x_{t+dt} = x_{t} + \text{drift} + \text{noise}\)). We will use this result to test the validity of our analytical solution. The following python code simulates 10,000 paths governed by this SDE and gives us the mean and standard deviation at \(t=10\) so we can compare with our analytical solution:

    import numpy as np

def simulate_ground_truth(mu=0.05, sigma=0.4, T=10.0, N_steps=1000, n_paths=10000, x0=1.0):
    """
    Simulate step-by-step SDE paths to find the true empirical moments.
    """
    dt = T / N_steps

    # Generate Brownian increments for 10,000 parallel paths
    dW = np.random.normal(0, np.sqrt(dt), (n_paths, N_steps))

    # Step-by-step numerical simulation (final values at T)
    increments_sim = 1 + mu * dt + sigma * dW
    x_sim_T = x0 * np.prod(increments_sim, axis=1)

    return np.mean(x_sim_T), np.std(x_sim_T)

# Set seed for reproducibility and run the simulation
np.random.seed(42)
sim_mean, sim_std = simulate_ground_truth()

    Plugging \(\mu=0.05, \sigma=0.4, t=10\) into the formulas we obtained we get:

    Method Expected Mean Expected Std Dev
    Numerical Simulation 1.6147 3.1937
    Analytical Formula 3.6693 7.2954
    Error +127.2% +128.4%

    A 127% error isn't a rounding issue, something is wrong in our analytical solution. What is the problem?

    The Missing Term

    The issue lies in how we compute the differential of a function. When we applied classical calculus to say that \(d(\ln x_t) = \frac{1}{x_t} dx_t\), we were implicitly relying on a first-order Taylor expansion.

    For any smooth function \(f(x)\), the exact change over a tiny step \(dx\) is given by the full Taylor series:

    \[df = f(x+dx) - f(x) = f'(x)dx + \frac{1}{2}f''(x)(dx)^2 + \frac{1}{6}f'''(x)(dx)^3 + \dots\]

    In classical calculus, we usually assume \(x_t\) is a smooth, differentiable function of time. This means its change over a small interval \(dt\) is strictly governed by its derivative: \(dx_t = x'(t)dt\).

    If we substitute this into the Taylor expansion, the second-order term becomes \((dx_t)^2 = (x'(t))^2 (dt)^2\). As the time step \(dt\) shrinks to zero, \((dt)^2\) goes to zero much faster than \(dt\). The higher-order terms become negligible, leaving us with the familiar first-order differential: \(df = f'(x)dx\).

    But our SDE does not describe a smooth function. It describes a path driven by Brownian motion, and Brownian motion is nowhere differentiable. Look back at the properties of \(dW_t\): a typical Brownian step scales with the square root of time (\(dW_t \sim \sqrt{dt}\)).

    This changes the geometry of our calculus entirely. If we square a Brownian increment, we don't get something infinitely smaller than \(dt\). We just get \(dt\):

    \[(dW_t)^2 = dt\]

    This is the central trick of stochastic calculus. Because the noise term scales with \(\sqrt{dt}\), the second-order Taylor term no longer vanishes.

    Applying these ideas to \(f(x) = \ln(x)\), the first two derivatives of our function are \(f'(x) = \frac{1}{x}\) and \(f''(x) = -\frac{1}{x^2}\). Plugging these into our second-order Taylor series gives us:

    \[d(\ln x_t) = \frac{1}{x_t} dx_t - \frac{1}{2x_t^2} (dx_t)^2\]

    To solve this, we first need to figure out what \((dx_t)^2\) is. Let's carefully square our SDE (\(dx_t = \mu x_t dt + \sigma x_t dW_t\)):

    \[(dx_t)^2 = (\mu x_t dt + \sigma x_t dW_t)^2 = \mu^2 x_t^2 (dt)^2 + 2\mu \sigma x_t^2 (dt)(dW_t) + \sigma^2 x_t^2 (dW_t)^2\]

    Now we apply our rules. Any power of \(dt\) greater than \(1\) becomes negligible, so \((dt)^2 \to 0\) and \((dt)(dW_t) \to 0\). Since \((dW_t)^2 = dt\), this term does not vanish. The entire expression collapses to:

    \[(dx_t)^2 = \sigma^2 x_t^2 dt\]

    Now we have everything we need. We substitute \(dx_t\) and \((dx_t)^2\) back into our Taylor expansion for \(d(\ln x_t)\):

    \[d(\ln x_t) = \frac{1}{x_t} \Big( \mu x_t dt + \sigma x_t dW_t \Big) - \frac{1}{2x_t^2} \Big( \sigma^2 x_t^2 dt \Big) = \mu dt + \sigma dW_t - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 dt = \left( \mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \right) dt + \sigma dW_t\]

    Integrating both sides from \(0\) to \(t\) (and remembering that \(W_0 = 0\)) yields:

    \[\ln(x_t) - \ln(x_0) = \left( \mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 \right) t + \sigma W_t\]

    Exponentiating, we get the final solution:

    \[x_t = x_0 e^{\left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma W_t}\]

    With our newly derived formula, let's run the exact same reality check we did before. Our corrected analytical solution is:

    \[x_t = x_0 e^{\left(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2\right)t + \sigma W_t}\]

    Using the exact same Moment Generating Function trick (\(\mathbb{E}[e^{\sigma W_t}] = e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t}\)), let's calculate the expected mean of our new formula:

    \[\mathbb{E}[x_t] = \mathbb{E}\left[x_0 e^{(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t + \sigma W_t}\right] = x_0 e^{(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t} \cdot \mathbb{E}[e^{\sigma W_t}] = x_0 e^{(\mu - \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2)t} \cdot e^{\frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 t} = x_0 e^{\mu t}\]

    We can compute the standard deviation the same way as before, obtaining:

    \[\text{StdDev}(x_t) = x_0 e^{\mu t} \sqrt{e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1} = \mathbb{E}[x_t]\sqrt{e^{\sigma^2 t} - 1}\]

    Now for the final test. If we plug our parameters (\(\mu=0.05, \sigma=0.4, t=10\)) into our corrected formulas, how do they compare against the numerical simulation?

    Method Expected Mean Expected Std Dev
    Numerical Simulation 1.6147 3.1937
    Corrected Analytical Formula 1.6487 3.2779
    Error 2.0% 2.5%

    The massive error is gone! The analytical solution now matches the numerical simulation. By adding the correction term, coming from the second order Taylor expansion, we fixed our analytical solution. What we just derived from scratch is an instance of a much broader result: Itô's Lemma.

    Itô's Lemma

    Itô's Lemma is the stochastic equivalent of the classical chain rule. Suppose we have a general stochastic process \(x_t\) governed by a drift term and a volatility term:

    \[dx_t = \mu(x,t) dt + \sigma(x,t) dW_t\]

    If we apply a twice-differentiable function \(f(t, x_t)\) to this process, Itô's Lemma states that the full differential \(df\) is:

    \[df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} dt + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} dx_t + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} (dx_t)^2\]

    Because we know that \((dW_t)^2 = dt\), we can substitute \((dx_t)^2 = \sigma(x,t)^2 dt\) directly into the equation. Grouping the deterministic \(dt\) terms and the random \(dW_t\) terms together gives us:

    \[df = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \mu(x,t) \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma(x,t)^2 \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} \right) dt + \sigma(x,t) \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} dW_t\]

    A very common and useful special case occurs when our underlying process is simply standard Brownian motion itself. If we apply Itô's Lemma to a function \(f(t, W_t)\), we have \(\mu=0\) and \(\sigma=1\). The equation reduces to:

    \[df(t, W_t) = \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial W^2} \right) dt + \frac{\partial f}{\partial W} dW_t\]

    We can use Itô's Lemma to actually solve Stochastic Differential Equations. Let's look at two examples.

    Example 1: Integrating Brownian Motion

    Suppose we want to find the integral \(\int_0^t W_s dW_s\). Classical calculus tells us \(\int x dx = \frac{1}{2}x^2\). Let's see what happens in stochastic calculus.

    We can treat standard Brownian motion as its own SDE: \(dx_t = 0 dt + 1 dW_t\) (so \(\mu=0\) and \(\sigma=1\)).

    Let's apply Itô's Lemma to the function \(f(t, x) = x^2\). The derivatives are \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = 0\), \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 2x\), and \(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = 2\). Plugging these into the equation gives:

    \[d(x_t^2) = \left( 0 + (0)(2x_t) + \frac{1}{2}(1)^2(2) \right) dt + (1)(2x_t) dW_t\]

    Putting \(x_t=W_t\) we get:

    \[d(W_t^2) = dt + 2W_t dW_t\]

    If we integrate both sides from \(0\) to \(t\) and rearrange, we get the exact analytical solution:

    \[\int_0^t W_s dW_s = \frac{1}{2}W_t^2 - \frac{1}{2}t\]

    The classical rule missed the \(-\frac{1}{2}t\) entirely.

    Example 2: The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Process

    SDEs don't just model exponential growth; they also model mean-reverting systems like interest rates or physical particles attached to springs. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is defined as:

    \[dx_t = -\theta x_t dt + \sigma dW_t\]

    To solve this, we can borrow the "integrating factor" trick from classical ODEs. Let's apply Itô's Lemma to the function \(f(t, x_t) = x_t e^{\theta t}\).

    The derivatives are \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = \theta x_t e^{\theta t}\), \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = e^{\theta t}\), and \(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = 0\). Using Itô's formula:

    \[d(x_t e^{\theta t}) = \theta x_t e^{\theta t} dt + e^{\theta t} dx_t + 0\]

    Substitute our SDE (\(dx_t = -\theta x_t dt + \sigma dW_t\)) into the equation:

    \[d(x_t e^{\theta t}) = \theta x_t e^{\theta t} dt + e^{\theta t} (-\theta x_t dt + \sigma dW_t)\]

    The drift terms cancel out:

    \[d(x_t e^{\theta t}) = \sigma e^{\theta t} dW_t\]

    Integrating both sides from \(0\) to \(t\) gives us the explicit solution for the process:

    \[x_t e^{\theta t} - x_0 = \sigma \int_0^t e^{\theta s} dW_s\]
    \[x_t = x_0 e^{-\theta t} + \sigma \int_0^t e^{-\theta(t-s)} dW_s\]

    Example 3: The Brownian Bridge

    Suppose you want a stochastic process that starts at \(0\) and is guaranteed to end exactly at \(0\) at a specific future time, say \(t=1\). How do we formulate an SDE for it, and how do we solve it?

    To ensure the process arrives exactly at zero at \(t=1\), we need a drift term that pulls the path back to zero. As the deadline approaches, that pull needs to become infinitely strong. This intuition gives us the SDE for a Brownian bridge:

    \[dx_t = -\frac{x_t}{1-t} dt + dW_t\]

    Let's look at the mechanics of this equation. The \(dW_t\) is our standard noise; the \(-\frac{x_t}{1-t} dt\) is a time-dependent restoring force.The \(-x_t\) numerator acts like a spring: if the path wanders into positive territory, the drift is negative and pulls it down; if the path is negative, the drift pulls it up. The \(1-t\) denominator is the deadline mechanism. When \(t\) is close to \(0\), the pull is gentle. But as \(t\) approaches \(1\), the denominator approaches zero, causing the strength of the restoring force to explode to infinity.

    We can solve this equation using another clever integrating factor. Let's apply Itô's Lemma to the function \(f(t, x_t) = \frac{x_t}{1-t}\).First, we compute the derivatives:\(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t} = \frac{x_t}{(1-t)^2}\), \(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \frac{1}{1-t}\), and \(\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2} = 0\).

    Plugging these into Itô's equation gives:

    \[d\left(\frac{x_t}{1-t}\right) = \frac{x_t}{(1-t)^2} dt + \frac{1}{1-t} dx_t + 0\]

    Now, substitute our original SDE (\(dx_t = -\frac{x_t}{1-t} dt + dW_t\)) in place of \(dx_t\):

    \[d\left(\frac{x_t}{1-t}\right) = \frac{x_t}{(1-t)^2} dt + \frac{1}{1-t} \left( -\frac{x_t}{1-t} dt + dW_t \right)\]

    Expand the terms on the right:

    \[d\left(\frac{x_t}{1-t}\right) = \frac{x_t}{(1-t)^2} dt - \frac{x_t}{(1-t)^2} dt + \frac{1}{1-t} dW_t = \frac{1}{1-t} dW_t\]

    Now we just integrate both sides from \(0\) to \(t\). Since our starting point is \(x_0 = 0\), the left side evaluates to:

    \[\frac{x_t}{1-t} - 0 = \int_0^t \frac{1}{1-s} dW_s\]

    Multiply the \((1-t)\) to the other side to get our explicit analytical solution:

    \[x_t = (1-t) \int_0^t \frac{1}{1-s} dW_s\]

    Look at the structure of the solution. Because of the \((1-t)\) term sitting out in front, as \(t \to 1\), the entire expression is forced mathematically to exactly \(0\). The stochastic noise is still there inside the integral, but it is ultimately killed by the \((1-t)\) term.

    A Final Remark: The Integral Problem

    Notice that our explicit solution for the Brownian bridge, just like our solution for the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, still contains an integral: \(\int_0^t \frac{1}{1-s} dW_s\).

    Throughout this text, we wrote down integrals of the form \(\int (\dots) dW_t\). We manipulated them and solved equations by treating \(dW_t\) as if it were just a normal variable of integration from classical calculus.

    But there is a problem: standard Riemann integral strictly requires the integrating variable to have bounded variation, meaning the path must have a finite length. As we established earlier, a Brownian path is infinitely jagged. Its total variation is infinite. If you try to apply classical Riemann-Stieltjes integration to \(dW_t\), the sum diverges to infinity.

    So what does it actually mean to compute \(\int (\dots) dW_t\)?

    Defining that integral rigorously requires an entirely new framework called the Itô Integral, which I decided to omit so we could get to solving SDEs in a more direct way.

    All you need to believe about the Itô Integral for this post is that such an integral exists, and that the fundamental theorem of calculus still holds for Itô Integrals: \(\int_0^t d(f(W_s)) = f(W_t) - f(W_0)\).
