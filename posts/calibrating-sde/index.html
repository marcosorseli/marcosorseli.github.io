    Fitting Stochastic Differential Equations

    In my process of learning SDEs, I have looked into many introductory textbooks and lecture notes, and I have found very little about how to actually calibrate these models to data. Most texts spend chapters proving existence and uniqueness or solving equations with Itô's Lemma, assuming the drift and volatility parameters are already given. In practice, however, all we ever observe is a sequence of discrete data points, and we have to work backward to find the parameters that generated them.

    My plan for this post is to walk through how that calibration works from scratch. We will simulate a path from a known SDE, derive an analytical method to recover its parameters via Maximum Likelihood, and then compare that approach against a machine learning approach by training a neural network on the exact same data.

    Generating Data

    Instead of starting with real-world data, we will start with an easier experiment: artificially generated data. That way we know the right answer and can check how close our estimates get. We will pick an SDE with known parameters, generate a synthetic path from it, and then see how well we can recover those parameters from the data points alone.

    As our test case, we will use the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process, which models mean-reverting systems:

    \[dX_t = \theta(\mu - X_t)dt + \sigma dW_t\]

    Here, \(\mu\) is the long-term mean, \(\theta\) is the speed of mean reversion, and \(\sigma\) is the volatility.

    Now we face our first problem: how do we actually generate a path from this equation? The SDE is written in continuous time using infinitesimal changes \(dt\) and \(dW_t\), whereas a computer can only step forward in discrete time increments of size \(\Delta t\).

    Euler-Maruyama vs. Exact Solution

    The usual approach to simulating an SDE is using an Euler-Maruyama discretization. Even though it has an ugly name, it is quite simple. Splitting our interval into steps of size \(\Delta t\), we can approximate the evolution of our equation by substituting \(dX_t\) with \(X_{t+\Delta t} - X_t\), \(dt\) with \(\Delta t\), and \(dW_t\) with \(\sqrt{\Delta t} Z\), where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\). This gives us:

    \[X_{t+\Delta t} = X_t + \theta(\mu - X_t)\Delta t + \sigma \sqrt{\Delta t} Z\]

    which we can iterate step-by-step to generate our paths. The smaller we pick \(\Delta t\), the more precise it gets.

    Since the OU equation has an analytical solution, we can also use the exact solution to generate paths. By applying Itô's Lemma (derived in the previous post), we get:

    \[X_t = X_0 e^{-\theta t} + \mu(1 - e^{-\theta t}) + \sigma \int_0^t e^{-\theta(t-s)} dW_s\]

    This equation tells us the exact distribution of the process at any time \(t\).

    First, assuming a known starting point \(X_0\), the first two terms are entirely deterministic, and since the expected value of an Itô integral is zero, we get:

    \[\mathbb{E}[X_t] = X_0 e^{-\theta t} + \mu(1 - e^{-\theta t})\]

    Second, the only randomness comes from the \(\sigma \int_0^t e^{-\theta(t-s)} dW_s\) term. We know that the Itô integral of a deterministic function against Brownian motion is normally distributed, and we can calculate its variance as (see Appendix A):

    \[\text{Var}(X_t) = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta t})\]

    Because \(X_t\) is the sum of a deterministic constant and a normal random variable, the process itself is normally distributed for any time \(t\):

    \[X_t \sim \mathcal{N}\left( X_0 e^{-\theta t} + \mu(1 - e^{-\theta t}), \, \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta t}) \right)\]

    Applying this equation on a step from \(t\) to \(t + \Delta t\), we get:

    \[X_{t+\Delta t} = X_t e^{-\theta \Delta t} + \mu(1 - e^{-\theta \Delta t}) + \sigma \sqrt{\frac{1 - e^{-2\theta \Delta t}}{2\theta}} Z\]

    where \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\).

    Comparing the Two Methods

    How do we choose which method to use?

    The following code generates a sequence of standard normal samples (\(Z\)) and applies it to both Euler-Maruyama and the exact solution method across \(10,000\) paths. After building up the paths, we compare the empirical distribution of values at \(t=1000\) of the two methods against the theoretical probability distribution of \(X_{1000}\):

    import numpy as np

# 1. Define Parameters
theta = 2.0
mu = 0.0
sigma = 1.5
X0 = 0.0
T = 1000.0
dt = 0.8  # Large time step to expose discretization bias
n_steps = int(T / dt)
n_paths = 10000

# Arrays to hold the terminal values
X_em = np.full(n_paths, X0)
X_exact = np.full(n_paths, X0)

# Pre-calculate exact transition terms
decay = np.exp(-theta * dt)
exact_std = np.sqrt((sigma**2 / (2 * theta)) * (1 - np.exp(-2 * theta * dt)))

# 2. Simulate Paths
for _ in range(n_steps):
    Z = np.random.normal(0, 1, n_paths)

    # Euler-Maruyama step
    X_em = X_em + theta * (mu - X_em) * dt + sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * Z

    # Exact step
    X_exact = X_exact * decay + mu * (1 - decay) + exact_std * Z

# 3. Theoretical Distribution at t = 1000
theoretical_mean = X0 * np.exp(-theta * T) + mu * (1 - np.exp(-theta * T))
theoretical_var = (sigma**2 / (2 * theta)) * (1 - np.exp(-2 * theta * T))
theoretical_std = np.sqrt(theoretical_var)

    Plotting the histograms of X_em and X_exact against the theoretical density gives:

    Distribution of \(X_{1000}\): Euler-Maruyama vs. Exact Solution
    Dashed: True Density | Orange: Exact Solution Histogram | Blue: Euler-Maruyama Histogram

    We can see that the exact solution method is much more accurate than Euler-Maruyama, getting very close to the true density. This divergence happens because Euler-Maruyama replaces the exponential decay \(e^{-\theta \Delta t}\) with the linear approximation \(1 - \theta \Delta t\) and approximates the step variance \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta \Delta t})\) with \(\sigma^2 \Delta t\). With a large step size (\(\Delta t = 0.8\)), those approximations distort the dynamics of the path, requiring much smaller steps to stay accurate.

    So from now on we will generate paths from the exact transition distribution. This isn’t special to OU: whenever an SDE has a known transition distribution, sampling from it directly beats Euler-Maruyama.

    Fitting Models

    Suppose we have a path of discrete observations \(X_0, X_1, \dots, X_N\) sampled at a constant time interval \(\Delta t\). How do we fit an OU model to this data?

    First, let's generate a single Ornstein-Uhlenbeck path with known parameters to serve as our ground truth. We simulate \(1,000\) steps of size \(\Delta t = 0.1\) (a total time of \(T = 100\)) with mean-reversion speed \(\theta = 0.1\), long-term mean \(\mu = 0.0\), and volatility \(\sigma = 1.0\):

    import numpy as np

# 1. Define True Parameters
theta_true = 0.1
mu_true = 0.0
sigma_true = 1.0
dt = 0.1
n_steps = 1000
X0 = 0.0

# 2. Generate the Exact Path
np.random.seed(13)
Z = np.random.normal(0, 1, n_steps)
X = np.zeros(n_steps + 1)
X[0] = X0

decay = np.exp(-theta_true * dt)
exact_std = np.sqrt((sigma_true**2 / (2 * theta_true)) * (1 - np.exp(-2 * theta_true * dt)))

for t in range(1, n_steps + 1):
    X[t] = X[t-1] * decay + mu_true * (1 - decay) + exact_std * Z[t-1]

    Plotting the generated path gives:

    Simulated Ornstein-Uhlenbeck Path
    Gray: True Mean (\(\mu = 0.0\)) | Orange: Simulated Path (\(X_t\))

    The Likelihood of a Path

    Now that we have data, the goal of calibration is to find the hidden parameters \(\theta\), \(\mu\), and \(\sigma\) using nothing but our observed data points \(x_0, x_1, \dots, x_N\). Of course, we can’t recover the true parameters with 100% certainty from a single finite path, but we can find the parameters that make our observed path as likely as possible.

    Here we run into a small problem: because the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process takes continuous values, the probability of hitting any single, exact number \(x_i\) is zero. To make sense of the "probability of a path," we instead look at a tiny, infinitesimal interval \(I_i = [x_i, x_i + dx_i]\) around each observed data point. If \(f(x)\) is the probability density function of the random variable \(X_i\), then the probability of landing inside that tiny interval is:

    \[P(X_i \in I_i) = \int_{x_i}^{x_i + dx_i} f(x) \, dx \approx f(x_i) \, dx_i\]

    With this in mind, the probability of observing our trajectory is the joint probability of the process passing through all of these tiny intervals one after another: \(P(X_0 \in I_0, X_1 \in I_1, \dots, X_N \in I_N)\).

    By the standard chain rule of probability, we can factor this joint probability into a sequence of conditional probabilities:

    \[P(X_0 \in I_0, \dots, X_N \in I_N) = P(X_0 \in I_0) \cdot P(X_1 \in I_1 \mid X_0 \in I_0) \cdots P(X_N \in I_N \mid X_{N-1} \in I_{N-1}, \dots, X_0 \in I_0)\]

    In general, calculating a massive chain of conditional dependencies like this is really hard. However, the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process is a Markov process, which means its transition to the next state depends only on its current state, not on the history of how it got there. Because our intervals \(I_i\) are infinitesimally narrow, knowing that \(X_3\) landed in \(I_3\) essentially fixes its value as \(x_3\) (up to \(dx_3\)). So a term like \(P(X_4 \in I_4 \mid X_3 \in I_3, X_2 \in I_2, X_1 \in I_1, X_0 \in I_0)\) is well-approximated by just \(P(X_4 \in I_4 \mid X_3 \in I_3)\), and the probability of the whole path occurring simplifies to:

    \[P(X_0 \in I_0, \dots, X_N \in I_N) \approx P(X_0 \in I_0) \prod_{i=1}^N P(X_i \in I_i \mid X_{i-1} \in I_{i-1})\]

    With a large \(N\), the initial \(P(X_0 \in I_0)\) factor barely matters next to the \(N\) transitions that follow, so we simply condition on the first observation:

    \[P(X_1 \in I_1, \dots, X_N \in I_N \mid X_0 \in I_0) \approx \prod_{i=1}^N P(X_i \in I_i \mid X_{i-1} \in I_{i-1})\]

    Now, if we write \(f(x_i \mid x_{i-1})\) for the transition density from \(x_{i-1}\) to \(x_i\), we can approximate each probability in the product over our infinitesimal intervals as \(P(X_i \in I_i \mid X_{i-1} \in I_{i-1}) \approx f(x_i \mid x_{i-1}) \, dx_i\). Plugging this in gives us:

    \[P(X_1 \in I_1, \dots, X_N \in I_N \mid X_0 \in I_0) \approx \left( \prod_{i=1}^N f(x_i \mid x_{i-1}) \right) dx_1 dx_2 \cdots dx_N\]

    Remember that our goal is to find the parameters \(\theta\), \(\mu\), and \(\sigma\) that maximize this probability. Notice that the infinitesimals \(dx_1 dx_2 \cdots dx_N\) don’t depend on the parameters, so maximizing the probability is the same as maximizing the function:

    \[\mathcal{L}(\theta, \mu, \sigma) = \prod_{i=1}^N f(x_i \mid x_{i-1})\]

    We call \(\mathcal{L}(\theta, \mu, \sigma)\) the Likelihood function, written this way to emphasize that the data points are fixed and we are treating the expression as a function of the parameters \(\theta\), \(\mu\), and \(\sigma\).

    Solving for the Parameters via Linear Algebra

    From the previous section, we already know the exact distribution of each step:

    \[X_i \mid X_{i-1} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( X_{i-1} e^{-\theta \Delta t} + \mu(1 - e^{-\theta \Delta t}), \, \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta \Delta t}) \right)\]

    To keep the algebra cleaner, let's introduce three variables:

    \[a = e^{-\theta \Delta t}, \quad b = \mu(1 - e^{-\theta \Delta t}), \quad s^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta \Delta t})\]

    With this substitution, the transition distribution is just \(\mathcal{N}(a X_{i-1} + b, \, s^2)\). Writing out the Gaussian density explicitly for each step \(f(x_i \mid x_{i-1})\) and plugging it into our Likelihood function gives:

    \[\mathcal{L}(a, b, s^2) = \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi s^2}} \exp\left( -\frac{(x_i - a x_{i-1} - b)^2}{2s^2} \right)\]

    Since the product of exponentials is the exponential of a sum, we can simplify this product into a single expression:

    \[\mathcal{L}(a, b, s^2) = \left(2\pi s^2\right)^{-N/2} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2s^2} \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - a x_{i-1} - b)^2 \right)\]

    Notice that \(a\) and \(b\) only appear in the sum inside the exponent, with a minus sign out front. The exponential is increasing and the factor \((2\pi s^2)^{-N/2}\) is positive, so for any fixed \(s^2 > 0\), maximizing \(\mathcal{L}\) over \(a\) and \(b\) comes down to minimizing the sum of squared errors:

    \[S(a, b) = \sum_{i=1}^N (x_i - a x_{i-1} - b)^2\]

    If we organize our observed data points and parameters into vectors and a matrix:

    \[Y = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \\ \vdots \\ x_N \end{bmatrix}, \quad M = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & x_0 \\ 1 & x_1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{N-1} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \beta = \begin{bmatrix} b \\ a \end{bmatrix}\]

    then each term \((x_i - a x_{i-1} - b)\) is just the \(i\)-th entry of the vector \(Y - M\beta\). Our sum of squares \(S(a, b)\) is simply the squared Euclidean norm of this vector:

    \[S(\beta) = \Vert Y - M\beta \Vert^2 = (Y - M\beta)^T (Y - M\beta) = Y^T Y - 2\beta^T M^T Y + \beta^T M^T M \beta\]

    To find the minimum, we take the gradient with respect to \(\beta\) and set it equal to zero:

    \[\nabla_\beta S(\beta) = -2 M^T Y + 2 M^T M \beta = 0 \implies M^T M \beta = M^T Y\]

    This is the classic Normal Equation from linear algebra. As long as our observed path is not completely flat (meaning the points \(x_0, \dots, x_{N-1}\) are not all identical), the columns of \(M\) are linearly independent and the \(2 \times 2\) matrix \(M^T M\) is invertible. Multiplying by its inverse gives us our optimal estimators \(\hat{b}\) and \(\hat{a}\) in a single step:

    \[\hat{\beta} = \begin{bmatrix} \hat{b} \\ \hat{a} \end{bmatrix} = (M^T M)^{-1} M^T Y\]

    Now that we have \(\hat{a}\) and \(\hat{b}\), we can plug the minimized sum of squares \(S(\hat{\beta}) = \Vert Y - M\hat{\beta} \Vert^2\) back into our likelihood function and differentiate with respect to \(s^2\) to find the optimal variance:

    \[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (s^2)} = (2\pi)^{-N/2} \exp\left( -\frac{S(\hat{\beta})}{2s^2} \right) \left[ -\frac{N}{2}(s^2)^{-\frac{N}{2} - 1} + (s^2)^{-\frac{N}{2}} \frac{S(\hat{\beta})}{2(s^2)^2} \right] = 0\]

    Since the exponential and power terms are strictly positive, the expression in the brackets must be zero, giving:

    \[\hat{s}^2 = \frac{1}{N} S(\hat{\beta}) = \frac{1}{N} \Vert Y - M\hat{\beta} \Vert^2\]

    To recover the original continuous-time Ornstein-Uhlenbeck parameters, we just undo the substitutions:

    \[\hat{\theta} = -\frac{\ln(\hat{a})}{\Delta t}, \quad \hat{\mu} = \frac{\hat{b}}{1 - \hat{a}}, \quad \hat{\sigma} = \sqrt{ \frac{2\hat{\theta} \hat{s}^2}{1 - \hat{a}^2} }\]

    So the whole calibration boils down to a bit of linear algebra, which takes a few lines of Python:

    # 1. Construct the vector Y and matrix M
Y = X[1:]
M = np.column_stack((np.ones(n_steps), X[:-1]))

# 2. Solve the Normal Equation: beta_hat = (M^T M)^(-1) M^T Y
beta_hat = np.linalg.inv(M.T @ M) @ M.T @ Y
b_hat, a_hat = beta_hat

# 3. Compute the residual variance: s^2 = (1/N) ||Y - M * beta_hat||^2
residuals = Y - M @ beta_hat
s2_hat = (1 / n_steps) * (residuals.T @ residuals)

# 4. Undo the substitutions to recover the original parameters
theta_hat = -np.log(a_hat) / dt
mu_hat = b_hat / (1 - a_hat)
sigma_hat = np.sqrt((2 * theta_hat * s2_hat) / (1 - a_hat**2))

print(f"theta | True: {theta_true:.4f} | Estimated: {theta_hat:.4f}")
print(f"mu    | True: {mu_true:.4f} | Estimated: {mu_hat:.4f}")
print(f"sigma | True: {sigma_true:.4f} | Estimated: {sigma_hat:.4f}")

    Running this on our path gives the following estimates:

    theta | True: 0.1000 | Estimated: 0.1105
mu    | True: 0.0000 | Estimated: -0.6793
sigma | True: 1.0000 | Estimated: 0.9851

    Our estimates for \(\sigma\) (0.9851 vs. 1.0) and \(\theta\) (0.1105 vs. 0.1) are very close to the true parameters, while our estimate for the long-term mean \(\mu\) (-0.6793 vs. 0.0) is noticeably further off.

    Why is \(\hat{\mu}\) harder to estimate? Volatility \(\sigma\) is estimated from the size of the step-by-step fluctuations, so having \(1,000\) steps gives us plenty of information to recover it. Estimating the mean \(\mu\) depends on watching the process cross back and forth around its center of gravity many times. With a small mean-reversion speed (\(\theta = 0.1\)), the pull toward zero is quite weak. To get a more precise estimate of \(\mu\), we would need a longer time horizon that lets the path bounce around the mean more times.

    We can even put a number on this. A rough rule of thumb (I won’t derive it here) is that the typical error of \(\hat{\mu}\) is about \(\frac{\sigma}{\theta\sqrt{T}}\), where \(T = N\Delta t\) is the total time we watch the process. For our path, \(T = 100\), so that is \(\frac{1.0}{0.1\sqrt{100}} = 1.0\). An estimate of \(-0.6793\) is less than one standard error from the truth, which is about what we should expect.

    Comparing Against a Neural Network

    In the previous section, we took a classical approach to modeling: we started with a differential equation, solved it, and used the exact solution to fit the parameters to our data. Another approach that is very common when we have a sequence of data points is to throw a machine learning model directly at the data.

    The goal of this section is to test how that approach compares against our analytical model. Can a neural network learn the dynamics of the process on its own by only looking at our simulated path?

    Fitting our analytical model relied on three properties of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process:

    • The Markov property: Only the current state \(x_{i-1}\) matters for predicting \(x_i\).
    • A linear drift: The expected value of the next step is a simple straight line, \(a x_{i-1} + b\).
    • Constant variance: The random noise around that expectation is Gaussian with a fixed variance \(s^2\).

    We wouldn’t know these facts just by looking at the data, and neither will a neural network if we simply feed it the observed points. In that sense, this comparison is a bit unfair: knowing ahead of time that the ground-truth path was generated by an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process makes it much easier to fit a model of the exact same type. Still, running this experiment lets us see how an analytical approach compares against the usual machine learning approach of fitting a black-box neural network.

    Setting Up a Probabilistic LSTM

    How do we actually set up a neural network for this? Suppose we are at step \(i-1\) and want to predict the next value \(x_i\). Because we are not assuming the Markov property, we can’t just feed the single point \(x_{i-1}\) into the network. Instead, we feed it a window of the last \(k\) observations, which we will call \(H_{i-1} = (x_{i-k}, \dots, x_{i-1})\).

    For sequential time-series data, a standard machine learning choice is an LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) network. Usually, these networks take a window \(H_{i-1}\) and output a single number: the best guess \(\mu_w(H_{i-1})\) for the next point \(x_i\) (where \(w\) represents the weights of the network).

    In a noisy process, however, a single guess is never going to hit \(x_i\) exactly; there will always be some random error around our prediction:

    \[x_i = \mu_w(H_{i-1}) + \text{error}\]

    Unless we have more information about the process we are trying to model, we usually assume that the error is Gaussian with mean \(0\) and some variance \(s^2\). In our analytical model, we knew the variance \(s^2\) was the exact same constant at every step. Our network doesn’t know that, so we won’t assume it’s constant.

    Instead, for every window our network will produce two numbers: a predicted value \(\mu_w(H_{i-1})\) and a predicted variance \(s^2_w(H_{i-1})\). Saying that the error around \(\mu_w(H_{i-1})\) is Gaussian with variance \(s^2_w(H_{i-1})\) is the exact same thing as saying the next step is drawn from a Gaussian distribution:

    \[X_i \mid H_{i-1} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mu_w(H_{i-1}), \, s^2_w(H_{i-1})\right)\]

    How Do We Train the Network?

    Just like before, we want to find the weights that maximize the likelihood function:

    \[\mathcal{L}(w) = \prod_{i=k}^N \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi s^2_w(H_{i-1})}} \exp\left( -\frac{\left(x_i - \mu_w(H_{i-1})\right)^2}{2s^2_w(H_{i-1})} \right)\]

    To maximize this function, we can’t use the same trick from earlier where we reduced it to a squared error minimization. The problem is that the variance term outside the exponential now changes at every step and depends on the weights \(w\). Without that trick, we have to solve for the weights \(w\) numerically, and the usual approach is gradient descent.

    Now we hit another problem: running gradient descent directly on \(\mathcal{L}(w)\) can go badly on a computer. By the product rule, every term in the derivative \(\nabla_w \mathcal{L}(w)\) is multiplied by nearly \(N\) densities. When multiplying hundreds of potentially small numbers together, the computer rounds the result down to zero, stalling gradient descent.

    To fix this, we consider the logarithm of \(\mathcal{L}(w)\) instead. Because the logarithm is an increasing function, \(\ln \mathcal{L}(w)\) has its maximum at the exact same weights \(w\) as \(\mathcal{L}(w)\), and it turns our product into a sum, which is much better behaved numerically. Since gradient descent algorithms are written to minimize a function instead of maximizing it, we flip the sign and minimize \(-\ln \mathcal{L}(w)\). We also divide by the number of steps \(M = N - k + 1\) to get an average per step, giving us the Mean Negative Log-Likelihood (NLL):

    \[\text{NLL}(w) = -\frac{1}{M} \ln \mathcal{L}(w) = \frac{1}{2M} \sum_{i=k}^N \left( \ln\left(2\pi s^2_w(H_{i-1})\right) + \frac{\left(x_i - \mu_w(H_{i-1})\right)^2}{s^2_w(H_{i-1})} \right)\]

    Looking at the pieces inside the sum, we can understand the tradeoff the network is trying to balance. To make the function small, it wants to put its guess \(\mu_w\) close to the true point \(x_i\) so the squared error \((x_i - \mu_w)^2\) is small. It can also make the first term \(\ln(2\pi s^2_w)\) smaller by predicting a tiny variance \(s^2_w\). But there is a catch: \(s^2_w\) sits in the denominator of the second term, so shrinking it makes that error term blow up whenever \(\mu_w\) misses \(x_i\).

    Building and Testing the Model in Python

    Because a neural network has thousands of weights, judging it only on the \(1,000\) steps it was trained on is misleading: with that much capacity, it can just memorize the random jumps in our path.

    To see how well our models generalize to unseen data, we first extend our ground-truth path from step \(1,000\) to step \(2,000\). We will fit both models on the first \(1,000\) steps (In-Sample) and test them on the next \(1,000\) future steps (Out-of-Sample):

    import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F

# Extend our ground-truth path from step 1,000 to 2,000
np.random.seed(50)  # Do not use the same random seed as before
Z_future = np.random.normal(0, 1, n_steps)

X_full = np.zeros(2 * n_steps + 1)
X_full[:n_steps + 1] = X  # First 1,000 steps from earlier

for t, z in enumerate(Z_future, start=n_steps + 1):
    X_full[t] = X_full[t-1] * decay + mu_true * (1 - decay) + exact_std * z

    Next, we cut this path into sliding windows \(H_{i-1}\) of length \(k = 10\). Each window \((x_{i-10}, \dots, x_{i-1})\) is paired with the single next point \(x_i\) that we want to predict, and we split the pairs at step \(1,000\) into our training and test sets:

    k = 10
xs, ys = [], []
for i in range(k, len(X_full)):
    xs.append(X_full[i-k:i])
    ys.append(X_full[i])

W_seq = torch.tensor(np.array(xs), dtype=torch.float32).unsqueeze(-1)
Y_seq = torch.tensor(np.array(ys), dtype=torch.float32).unsqueeze(-1)

# Split into In-Sample (up to step 1,000) and Out-of-Sample (steps 1,001 to 2,000)
split = n_steps - k + 1
W_train, Y_train = W_seq[:split], Y_seq[:split]
W_test,  Y_test  = W_seq[split:], Y_seq[split:]

    Now we define the network itself. It uses two layers:

    • An LSTM layer: In the analytical model, we knew that the single value \(x_{i-1}\) was the only feature we needed. Here, the LSTM layer reads all \(10\) points in the window \(H_{i-1}\) and summarizes them into a vector of \(32\) features.
    • A Linear layer: Takes a linear combination of those \(32\) features to output two numbers. The first number is our predicted mean \(\mu_w(H_{i-1})\). Since the variance \(s^2_w(H_{i-1})\) inside our NLL formula must be positive, we pass the second number through the \(\text{Softplus}\) function, \(\ln(1 + e^z)\), which maps any real number to \((0, \infty)\).
    class ProbabilisticLSTM(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden_size=32):
        super().__init__()
        self.lstm = nn.LSTM(input_size=1, hidden_size=hidden_size, batch_first=True)
        self.fc = nn.Linear(hidden_size, 2)

    def forward(self, w_seq):
        out, _ = self.lstm(w_seq)
        features = out[:, -1, :]  # The 32 features summarizing each window
        params = self.fc(features)
        mu = params[:, 0:1]
        s2 = F.softplus(params[:, 1:2]) + 1e-6
        return mu, s2

    To train the network, we write out our \(\text{NLL}(w)\) equation in PyTorch and run gradient descent for \(500\) steps on the training windows. Note that I’m deliberately leaving out early stopping and weight decay, two standard tricks for keeping a network from memorizing its training data. I want to see what happens when a flexible model is left free to fit both the mean and the variance of a short, noisy path:

    def gaussian_nll(y, mu, s2):
    return 0.5 * torch.mean(torch.log(2 * np.pi * s2) + (y - mu)**2 / s2)

    Making Sense of the Results

    Notice that our Analytical MLE (0.2473) gets a slightly lower score on the training path than the Ground Truth parameters (0.2480) that generated it. This is expected: when fitting the model, we found the parameters that minimize NLL on those specific \(1,000\) steps. The neural network, however, gets a much smaller NLL (-0.0490).

    When we step into the future (steps \(1000\) to \(2000\)), the analytical model (0.2665) performs almost identically to the ground truth (0.2664), while the neural network NLL jumps to 1.8643, showing that it overfit the training data.

    We can see how this overfitting happens by plotting the step-by-step variance \(s^2_w(H_{i-1})\) predicted by the LSTM against the constant variance of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process:

    Predicted Step Variance: Analytical MLE vs. LSTM
    Gray: True / Analytical Variance (\(s^2 \approx 0.099\)) | Orange: LSTM Predicted Variance (\(s^2_w\)) | Blue Dashed: Train / Test Split (step \(1000\))

    We can see that even though the true process has a constant variance, the neural network learned a very jagged variance path, with a lot of jumps from one window to the next. This is another clear sign of overfitting: the high number of parameters allowed the network to memorize specific patterns of the path.

    Our analytical model, on the other hand, had only three numbers to learn. Because the math of the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process already gave us the exact structure of the process, the model had no room to memorize the noise, allowing it to generalize to the future almost as well as the true process itself.

    Remember that the data really is OU, so there was never anything left for a bigger model to find. Nothing can meaningfully beat the true parameters, and our three-number fit scores within \(0.0001\) of them on the test path. A flexible model only earns its keep when the dynamics are something three parameters can’t capture, like a nonlinear drift or a volatility that depends on the state, and there is enough data to learn it.

    Of course, we took a very naive approach by parameterizing the data directly with a standard sequence network. There are much better ways of doing this, and there is a whole field of Neural SDEs where people study how to combine the modeling capabilities of stochastic differential equations with the flexibility of neural networks. The following two preprints give an overview of Neural SDEs and how differential equations can be combined with deep learning for time-series modeling:

    • On Neural Differential Equations — Patrick Kidger (2022)
    • Comprehensive Review of Neural Differential Equations for Time Series Analysis — YongKyung Oh et al. (2025)

    Appendix A

    In the first section, we used the fact that the stochastic term \(I_t = \sigma \int_0^t e^{-\theta(t-s)} dW_s\) has variance \(\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta t})\). We can derive this directly from the definition of the Itô integral.

    Partition the time interval \([0, t]\) into \(n\) small steps \(0 = t_0 < t_1 < \dots < t_n = t\) of width \(\Delta s = t_k - t_{k-1}\). The Itô integral is the limit of the Riemann-like sum:

    \[I_t = \sigma \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n e^{-\theta(t - t_{k-1})} \Delta W_k\]

    where the Brownian increments \(\Delta W_k = W_{t_k} - W_{t_{k-1}}\) are independent random variables distributed as \(\mathcal{N}(0, \Delta s)\).

    Because the increments \(\Delta W_k\) are independent, the variance of the sum is simply the sum of the individual variances:

    \[\text{Var}(I_t) = \sigma^2 \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n \text{Var}\left( e^{-\theta(t - t_{k-1})} \Delta W_k \right)\]

    Pulling the deterministic factor out of the variance squares it, and substituting \(\text{Var}(\Delta W_k) = \Delta s\) gives:

    \[\text{Var}(I_t) = \sigma^2 \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n e^{-2\theta(t - t_{k-1})} \Delta s\]

    As \(n \to \infty\), this sum is the standard Riemann integral of \(e^{-2\theta(t-s)}\) from \(0\) to \(t\):

    \[\text{Var}(I_t) = \sigma^2 \int_0^t e^{-2\theta(t-s)} ds = \sigma^2 \left[ \frac{e^{-2\theta(t-s)}}{2\theta} \right]_{s=0}^{s=t} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1 - e^{-2\theta t})\]
